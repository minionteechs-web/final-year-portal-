# CHAPTER ONE  
## INTRODUCTION

### 1.1 Background to the Study

Universities exist to produce graduates who can think, solve problems, and contribute to society. That mission depends heavily on what happens between lecturer and student. Globally, higher education no longer treats teaching as a private classroom ritual. Student Evaluation of Teaching (SET), course feedback tools, and related quality mechanisms help institutions understand how instruction is experienced (Marsh, 1982; Spooren, Brockx, & Mortelmans, 2013). Teaching effectiveness is widely recognised as multidimensional—clarity, organisation, interaction, fairness, and related behaviours—rather than a single popularity score (Benton & Cashin, 2011).

Beside formal SET, students also seek advance information about instructors through peers and, in some contexts, public rating websites. Those informal channels show real demand for preknowledge, but they are often biased, extreme, or outdated (Kindred & Mohammed, 2005; Otto, Sanford, & Ross, 2008). The academic task is not to deny students’ need for orientation; it is to meet that need with credible, criterion-based systems.

In Nigeria, public universities have historically weighted staff appraisal toward research publications, qualifications, and service, while teaching effectiveness often receives weaker formal attention (Archibong & Nja, 2011). Student evaluation of teaching has entered institutional debate and some practice, yet adoption remains uneven (Adomi, 2007; Amini, 2018; Ani, 2020). Local instrument work shows that Nigerian students can rate teaching reliably when scales are carefully developed (Amuche & Umar, 2019), and many lecturers are not uniformly hostile to evaluation (Inko-Tariah, 2013). What remains scarce is a living, student-facing overview tool that converts ratings into early orientation.

Against that gap, campus oral culture thrives. Hostel conversations, senior advice, and departmental chat groups become the default lecturer overview for freshers. Students want to know who is interactive, who releases notes early, who is audible in a crowded hall, who is strict on punctuality, who marks fairly, and who is approachable. The desire is legitimate; the problem is reliability. Informal stories are selective and sticky. They produce two linked harms. First, many students underperform not only from weak ability, but because they do not know how to adjust to a particular lecturer’s style. Second, capable lecturers lose early goodwill among new intakes through inherited misconception and must rebuild reputation every semester even when their practice is consistent.

The Department of Computer Science, University of Nigeria, Nsukka (UNN), sits inside this reality: large classes, varied teaching personalities, and limited structured pre-course information. This project responds with an online students’ system for preknowledge and overview of lecturers—lecturer-validated qualities, an automated student form, and a PHP/MySQL portal with charts—so students prepare with evidence and lecturers maintain reputation by staying true to good practice.

### 1.2 Statement of the Problem

Students in the Department of Computer Science, UNN—especially freshers and newly registered course participants—often begin semesters without reliable, dimensional information about how their lecturers teach. The dominant substitute is informal peer narrative. Because that narrative is incomplete or outdated, students who could succeed by adapting study habits fail to match strategy to expectation. Parallel to this, capable lecturers suffer recurring reputational injury not always grounded in current performance.

Existing processes do not adequately close the gap. Formal appraisal may under-emphasise teaching relative to research (Archibong & Nja, 2011). Where feedback exists, it is often management-centred, paper-slow, or unavailable as continuous chart-driven overview for students. Related Nigerian web evaluation projects improve administrative capture (e.g., Onihunwa, Jeje, & Dada, 2020), yet few are designed as student preknowledge systems for UNN Computer Science.

There is therefore need for a web-based lecturer assessment and overview portal that:

1. is grounded in lecturer-validated assessment qualities;  
2. collects structured student responses automatically; and  
3. presents aggregated results as transparent charts for student preparation and formative lecturer feedback.

Without such a system, misconception will continue to shape academic behaviour more strongly than evidence.

### 1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study

**Aim**  
To design and develop an online students’ system for preknowledge and overview of lecturers in the Department of Computer Science, University of Nigeria, Nsukka.

**Specific Objectives**  
1. Ascertain lecturer assessment qualities through separate oral interviews with lecturers (with note-taking and later compilation), focusing on eight indicators: interactiveness, audibility, availability of lecture notes, class control, communication, punctuality, fairness in assessment, and approachability.  
2. Design an automated response form through which students can assess lecturers on the identified qualities.  
3. Develop a web-based lecturer assessment portal using PHP, with dashboards and charts that present aggregated overview information.

### 1.4 Significance of the Study

**Students** gain early insight into teaching patterns and can adjust study strategies with less dependence on rumour.  
**Lecturers** receive organised formative feedback and are less trapped in semester-to-semester reputational resets caused by misconception.  
**The Department of Computer Science and UNN** obtain a practical orientation and quality-assurance complement.  
**Future developers and researchers** receive a documented PHP/MySQL model for student-facing lecturer overview in a Nigerian computing department.

### 1.5 Scope and Limitations of the Study

The study is limited to the Department of Computer Science, UNN. It covers identification of eight lecturer assessment qualities, design of an automated student response form, and development of a PHP/MySQL portal with charts. Respondents are Computer Science lecturers and undergraduate students. The study does not cover all UNN faculties, does not replace official promotion appraisal, and does not measure research productivity. Student ratings may contain bias; anonymity and aggregation reduce misuse risk but do not eliminate all subjectivity. Interface diagrams and screenshots are placed in the appendices of the bound copy.

### 1.6 Operational Definition of Terms

**Preknowledge:** Useful information a student obtains about a lecturer’s teaching style before or early in a course.  
**Overview of Lecturers:** A chart-based summary of aggregated student ratings across defined quality indicators.  
**Lecturer Assessment Qualities:** Interactiveness, audibility, availability of lecture notes, class control, communication, punctuality, fairness in assessment, and approachability.  
**Automated Response Form:** A digital questionnaire that stores and processes student ratings without paper collation.  
**Web-Based Assessment Portal:** The PHP-driven site for registration, assessment, charts, and role-based dashboards.  
**Misconception:** An inaccurate or exaggerated belief about a lecturer, often transmitted across student cohorts.  
**Student Evaluation of Teaching (SET):** Organised collection of students’ judgements about instructional quality for feedback or quality assurance.
